## FASTQ and FASTQC

FASTQ is the file format that NGSs generates, While FASTQC is an computational approach for quality evaluation of your generated data.

Each sequence will contain:

```
sequence ID
sequence
quality ID
quality score # uses ASCII codes of +33 symbols,
              # eg.[worst] some symbols - numbers - capital letters - lowercase letters [best]
```
Phred quality scores quantify sequencing accuracy and are defined as:

$$Q = -10 \log_{10} P(\text{error})$$

where $P(\text{error})$ is the probability that a base is incorrectly called.

### Low quality score? What to do? - Quality Control Considerations for Sequencing Data

When evaluating sequencing data, it is important to first determine whether the data comes from **single-end** or **paired-end** reads, as this can influence downstream analysis and quality assessment.

Sequencing quality is not uniform across reads. In many platforms, **base quality tends to decrease toward the ends of reads** (especially the 3′ end), meaning later bases are often less reliable than earlier ones. A good quality read will be consistent and high-quality along the read and it will be higher than 30. Poor quality reads will have high variance and quality will drastically decrease in length.

Poor quality can indicate issues with **sample preparation**, and re-preparing or re-sequencing the sample may be necessary.

Quality control of the massive FASTQ files can be done by FASTQC [https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/]. FastQC provides summaries of:
- Per-base sequence quality
- GC content - Mean mean GC content should be within the expectation at around 45%. 
- Sequence duplication levels
- Adapter contamination

These metrics help identify potential problems before proceeding to downstream analysis.

## (The Incredibly Slow) Early Sequence Alignment Methods (1 with 1)

Alignment methods can be divided into two types:

---

### Global Alignment

Tries to align the **entire sequence** from end to end.

#### Needleman-Wunsch Algorithm

A **dynamic programming** pairwise sequence alignment algorithm and one of the earliest algorithms (developed in **1970**). The goals are to find functional, structural, and evolutionary relationships between sequences.

1. Scoring System
  
  | Event | Score |
  |-------|-------|
  | Match | +1 |
  | Mismatch | -1 |
  | Gap | -2 |

2. Matrix Filling

  Each cell in the scoring matrix is computed from three possible sources:
  
  | Arrow | Source | Calculation |
  |-------|--------|-------------|
  | ↑ Top | Cell directly above | Above score + gap penalty |
  | ← Left | Cell directly to the left | Left score + gap penalty |
  | ↖ Diagonal | Upper-left diagonal cell | Diagonal score + match/mismatch score |

  > The **highest** of the three values is placed into the table. If the highest value is negative, it is still kept (unlike Smith-Waterman). Ties can be broken arbitrarily and may result in multiple equally optimal alignments.

3. Traceback

  - Begins from the **bottom-right cell**
  - Follows the path of arrows back to the **top-left cell**
  - Reconstructs the optimal global alignment
  - Gaps are introduced wherever a horizontal (←) or vertical (↑) arrow is followed

4. Complexity

  $$O(m \times n)$$
  
  Where `m` and `n` are the lengths of the two sequences — which is why this method is considered slow for long sequences.

---

#### Local Alignment

Aligns regions having the **highest sequence similarities**, so substrings of the query and the target will be aligned. More suitable for **divergent sequences** or when only a conserved domain is shared.

#### Smith-Waterman Algorithm

Like Needleman-Wunsch, it is a **dynamic programming** algorithm but operates at a **local scale** (developed in **1981**).

Key Differences from Needleman-Wunsch

| Feature | Needleman-Wunsch | Smith-Waterman |
|---------|-----------------|----------------|
| Scope | Global | Local |
| Negative scores | Kept as-is | Reset to **0** |
| First row/col init | Cumulative gap penalties | All **0s** |
| Traceback start | Bottom-right corner | Highest-scoring cell anywhere |
| Traceback end | Top-left corner | Any cell with score **0** |
| Values considered per cell | Top, Left, Diagonal | Top, Left, Diagonal, **+ 0** |

> Setting negative values to **0** allows the algorithm to "restart," enabling it to find local regions of high similarity without being penalized by poorly aligned flanking regions.

Scoring System

| Event | Score |
|-------|-------|
| Match | +1 (or user-defined) |
| Mismatch | -1 (or user-defined) |
| Gap | -2 (or user-defined) |

Complexity

$$O(m \times n)$$

Similarly slow — which motivates the use of heuristic methods like **BLAST** and **FASTA** for large-scale database searches.

---

### Why These Methods Are Slow

Both Needleman-Wunsch and Smith-Waterman have **quadratic time complexity**:

- Aligning two sequences of length **1,000** → ~**1,000,000** operations
- For genomic-scale data (**billions** of base pairs) → computationally intractable

This limitation led to the development of faster **heuristic alignment tools** (covered in subsequent sections).
