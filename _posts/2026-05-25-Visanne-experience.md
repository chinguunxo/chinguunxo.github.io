This blog post is for anyone who is considering:
> - Starting Visanne  
> - Stopping it due to side effects  

## My Story

I was first introduced to Visanne during my first visit to an international hospital in China. It was honestly a refreshing experience. Compared to my previous encounters with doctors, they took my pain seriously instead of dismissing it with advice like “drink more water” or “exercise more.”

During that visit, I was diagnosed with heterogeneous thickening of the uterine wall, which is associated with adenomyosis. Although Visanne is officially prescribed for endometriosis, it is also used to manage adenomyosis-related pain because it suppresses the menstrual cycle. (This varies by individual, and some people still experience spotting.)

## Why I Stopped

For me, the side effects began after about a year of taking the pill. I became extremely unmotivated, irritable, and anxious, and I constantly felt the need to sleep.  

The turning point came when I found myself crying every single day and having suicidal thoughts over something as minor as an organic chemistry midterm result.  

Even then, deciding to stop was not easy. All I could remember was standing on a bus in -20°C weather, in unbearable pain, trying to make my way home alone.  

After discussing it with a doctor, I decided to stop taking Visanne. I needed to know whether what I was experiencing was truly a side effect or something else.

## Why I Went Back on It

Within a week of stopping, I noticed the depression lifting. I stopped obsessing over small things and finally felt relaxed. For the first time in a long while, I felt genuinely happy.  

I also read and watched other women’s experiences online, and many reported similar mental health side effects. The timing varied widely—some experienced them within weeks, others after months or even years. It seems highly individual.

Then my period returned about a month after stopping the pill—and the pain came back with it. It was intense. I needed more painkillers than I used to, and even then, they barely helped.  

The pain lasted for four days. I either skipped classes or showed up with a heating pad, unable to focus because of the brain fog. It took me nearly three weeks to fully catch up with my coursework. I remember thinking, “At least I’m happy.”  

But in the end, it was not worth constantly anticipating when the next episode of severe pain would strike or scrambling to figure out how to manage it alone.  

During my last cycle, my period started on the day of my statistics final exam. The painkillers I took had no effect, and about an hour into the exam, I stopped feeling my hands.  

Afterward, I asked my professor if I could retake the exam, explaining that I had been in severe pain. He responded, “Anyone could say that and ask to retake the exam,” and refused before walking away.  

I was left crying—not just out of frustration, but because I realized I might have to live with this for the rest of my life. I do not want to be limited by this pain.  

Planning my life around my cycle felt nearly impossible as a college student studying abroad. So, I made the decision to go back on the pill.

## What I Hope for the Future

I hope for better treatment options—ones that do not force a choice between physical pain and mental health.  

I hope for more understanding from people, especially in academic and professional environments, about what chronic pain conditions actually feel like.  

And most of all, I hope to one day live without having to constantly choose between two kinds of suffering. Until then I will work hard towards contributing to its research as much as I can.
